LLM Council Report

MJ Recruit: the whole play, counciled before Friday's pitch · 14 July 2026

The question: Is the whole MJ play right? The live site + run-sheet app, three-tier pricing (£199+£35 / £299+£69 / £399+£129), the midnight-rule no-show pitch, the growth plan, and Paul's position as worker, driver and would-be vendor, three days before a Zoom with the owner.

Chairman's verdict

Where the council agrees: localStorage on Dee's single phone is a live landmine with Paul's name on it; the growth plan is the wrong pitch for Friday (the owner's stated pain is £50–100 no-shows, not client acquisition); and the worker/vendor collision is social before it is contractual: the points system scores the coworkers Paul carpools with.

Where it clashes: Executor says keep the tiers and lead with tier 2 as a live demo. First Principles says the tiers are inverted: the run sheet IS the product and the website is a competence prop. The Expansionist's multi-agency SaaS vision was voted the biggest blind spot by every reviewer (Paul doesn't own the D&J relationship, MJ does), but its kernel survives: retain the IP.

Blind spots caught in review: Dee is the real decision-maker and her buy-in decides survival; nobody scripted the "just do it free" downside; "no contract" cuts both ways; and the relationship has to survive Monday whatever Friday brings.

The recommendation: Sell the run sheet, not the bundle. Open with the owner's own number: "You told me a no-show costs £50–100. Here's the tool that stops them." Demo live, present tier 2 as "what I built for you," site included as proof of polish. Growth plan moves to a post-yes email. Points system becomes "your policy, configurable by you." Bring one page of written terms separating software from driving work. Script the free-ask answer: "The free version was the demo; the £69 keeps it running and backed up." Show Dee before the owner. Ask for a start date on the call.

The one thing to do first Build the backup/export button tonight. One click, crew list and shift history saved out. It kills the unanimous number-one risk and turns "data on one phone" into "your data, backed up, yours."

Full advisor responses

The Contrarian Rated strongest 5/5

The localStorage single-phone architecture is the landmine, not a footnote. Dee's phone gets lost, dropped in a fryer, or replaced on a carrier upgrade and every crew list, shift history, and points record vanishes with zero backup. Coordinators' phones fail constantly and Paul has no migration/export/backup story built. When it happens, Paul, the vendor AND a driver who needs Dee to like him, eats the blame in person. Bigger problem: the midnight rule and points system formalise discipline against the exact coworkers he rides in the car with. First time the algorithm dings someone unfairly, it's Paul's app, Paul's fault, and Paul's carpool that gets awkward. Pricing is anchored fine against £5–15k agency quotes, but nobody has tested whether MJ's margins support £69–129/month, and "avoided cancellation pays 4 months" is Paul's framing, not Dee's real cancellation rate. And there is no separation clause: what happens if Paul leaves, gets busy, or MJ wants the software without the driver?

The First Principles Thinker

The owner isn't buying a website. He's buying relief from a pain he already quantified: £50–100 per no-show, chased manually by one overworked coordinator. That is the entire deal. The marketing site, testimonials and growth plan solve problems MJ never stated. The run sheet + midnight rule is the product; the marketing site is a proof-of-competence prop. The current tiers invert the value order, making the free-to-build website the entry tier and burying the thing that pays for itself in an upsell. And nobody asked whether MJ should grow: more clients means more coordination chaos for Dee before it means more margin. Fix the bottleneck first.

The Expansionist Voted biggest blind spot 5/5

Paul is pricing a coordinator tool when he's sitting on a staffing marketplace. MJ has relationships with D&J and Sanjay's, a pool of Leicester workers, and the pain of matching bodies to shifts under time pressure: the problem Deputy, Rota and every hospitality SaaS solves for billions. D&J's supply chain has multiple staffing agencies almost certainly running the same WhatsApp chaos. Structure the deal so Paul retains IP and can white-label the run-sheet system to other agencies later. That's the difference between a £129/mo side hustle and a vertical SaaS with a flagship reference customer. (Peer review consensus: right structure, wrong pitch for Friday: Paul doesn't own the D&J relationship, MJ does.)

The Outsider

Cold read: a guy who drives their van wants to sell them a £129/month system in three days. Is Dee going to think Paul built a tool to make her job trackable by management, using data she generates? He's proposing to instrument his own coworkers. Second: the owner's anchor isn't a £5–15k agency quote, it's "this cost nothing yesterday." Third: localStorage on one phone reads as "no backup, single point of failure, sold as a system." Fourth: no contract sounds trustworthy until you notice MJ can extract months of value, cancel, and Paul's still driving their van next weekend with no leverage.

The Executor

Walk in with a live URL, not a deck: demo the run sheet generating a real WhatsApp message in ninety seconds. Don't lead with three tiers on a Friday call with a non-technical owner: lead with tier 2 as "here's what I built for you," then show cheaper and fuller options. Cut the growth pitch entirely; save the stats for a follow-up email after the yes. Before Friday, add an export/backup button, a two-hour job that stops data loss becoming the story MJ tells about you. Ask for a start date and first payment on the call. Don't leave with "let me think about it."

Peer review highlights

What the reviews agreed on, and what everyone missed

Unanimous: the Contrarian's response was rated strongest by all five reviewers for connecting the technical, social and structural risks into one picture. The Expansionist's SaaS vision was voted the biggest blind spot by all five: wrong timing, wrong audience, and it never addressed the worker/vendor conflict.

What all five advisors missed (surfaced only in review): Paul's employment survives or sours regardless of Friday's outcome, and nobody war-gamed the awkward-no scenario. Dee's buy-in, not the owner's, determines whether the tool survives contact with reality, and her informal WhatsApp control is what's being automated. Nobody scripted the response to "just give me the site free." And "no contract" is also Paul's own exit ramp when the agency job starts.
